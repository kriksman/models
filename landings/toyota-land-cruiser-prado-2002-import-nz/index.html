Why Kiwis choose the Land Cruiser Prado

People look at the Prado because it is a proper body-on-frame Toyota SUV with full-time 4WD rather than a soft-roader setup. The 120 Series also brought the newer 1KD-FTV common-rail diesel, which matters because it was the newer diesel option at launch while the petrol 3RZ-FE and 5VZ-FE carried over from the earlier Prado. For buyers who want one vehicle for daily use, towing and rougher roads, that mix is a big part of the appeal.

Technical specifications02

Land Cruiser Prado technical specifications

The big draw with a 2002 Prado is not one single trim, it is the combination of Toyota 4WD hardware and a wide spread of usable engine choices. On J120 models, full-time 4WD came with a 2-speed transfer and a Torsen centre differential with centre diff lock. That setup gives you constant drive to all four wheels, then low range and a lockable centre diff when the surface gets loose or you are pulling weight. It is also one of those model lines where getting the exact chassis code right matters, because 2002 spans two Prado generations.

What sets it apart03

Features that stand out

On a Prado of this age, the worthwhile features are the ones that change how it works day to day, not the usual basic equipment list.

3-door or 5-door body choice

This generation was offered in both 3-door and 5-door form. The shorter body gives you a 2455mm wheelbase, while the 5-door stretches to 2790mm, so the choice changes parking ease, rear-seat access and touring space in a real way.

TX Limited grade in the range

TX Limited sits above the regular TX in the grade ladder, which is useful because it gives buyers a known higher-spec version to target when scanning auction cars. Exact equipment still needs confirming on the individual vehicle, but it is one of the trims worth understanding before you bid.

Long-wheelbase 5-door dimensions

The 5-door body measures 4715mm long and 1875mm wide, which puts it in the proper family-4WD bracket rather than compact-SUV territory. That extra length is part of why the Prado has remained popular with buyers who need one vehicle to do several jobs.

Short-wheelbase 3-door option

The 3-door version is 4340mm long, so it is meaningfully shorter than the 5-door. That is a useful detail if you want Prado running gear and 4WD hardware without the full-size footprint.

Fitted technology04

Under the skin

The Prado earns its reputation from the hardware underneath it, and this is the part worth understanding before choosing an engine or body style.

01

Full-time 4WD with Torsen centre differential

The J120 Prado used full-time 4WD rather than a part-time setup. A Torsen centre differential manages the front-to-rear drive split in normal use, then the centre diff lock can mechanically tie the axles together when the surface gets slippery. That gives the Prado a more settled feel on sealed roads than an old-school part-time system, because you are not switching between 2WD and 4WD. It is one of the practical reasons these work well as everyday SUVs as well as weekend tow or track vehicles.

02

2-speed transfer case

The transfer case gives you high range for normal driving and low range for slower, heavier work. Low range is not about speed, it is about control, especially when climbing, descending or pulling a load at low pace. Buyers who only look at engine size sometimes miss this, but the transfer case is a big part of what separates a Prado from a road-focused SUV. If you need a genuine 4WD tool, this matters more than a long options list.

03

Engine range at launch

The launch-era engine lineup covered three quite different buyers. The 3RZ-FE 2.7-litre petrol made 120kW, the 5VZ-FE 3.4-litre V6 made 136kW, and the 1KD-FTV 3.0-litre turbo-diesel made 120kW. The useful point here is that the diesel was the newer D-4D common-rail option for the 120 Series, while the petrol engines carried over from the earlier Prado. On an import search, that helps narrow the kind of Prado you actually want.

04

Model range split in 2002

This is one of those years where the production split matters as much as the badge. Early 2002 vehicles can still be the earlier Prado generation, while October 2002 marks the start of the J120 series. If you are aiming for the newer platform, checking the model code first is one of the simplest ways to avoid paying for the wrong vehicle.
